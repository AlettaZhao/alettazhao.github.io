A large field of saturated teal, finished with a reflective laminate. In the grey-and-white space of an airport, it is <mark>hard to ignore</mark>. The cover doesn't rely on a single image; typography alone builds the <mark>visual hierarchy</mark>.

## Colour

  <div><span style="background:#FFC09C"></span><code>#FFC09C</code></div>
  <div><span style="background:#FFFFFF"></span><code>#FFFFFF</code></div>
</div>

**Background.** Teal with a subtle light-to-dark gradient rather than a flat fill: <mark>eye-catching without crowding the type</mark>.

**Type.** Not pure orange but a pinkish, milky champagne. Type and background each take a step back from the extreme, giving <mark>strong contrast without glare</mark>.

**Accent.** White carries all the small text, <mark>bridging and cooling</mark> the space between the teal and the champagne.

**Overall.** It reads as a colour clash, but every colour has been softened. Striking, never aggressive.

## Type

<figure class="ds-fig ds-narrow">
  <img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="/images/blog/ds01-type.jpg" alt="The cover on black, isolating the typography">
</figure>

**Headline: Linotype Didot Pro.** A high-contrast serif with built-in fashion-magazine glamour. It <mark>sets the tone</mark> for the whole cover.

**Supporting text: Eina 02.** A modern sans-serif that keeps the magazine name, issue number and other small text <mark>legible</mark>.

**Size contrast.** The jump in size between headline and supporting text is bold, so the <mark>hierarchy</mark> is obvious at a glance. The small text is pushed to the edge of visibility: it never competes with the headline, but it's there when you look for it.

<figure class="ds-fig ds-inline">
  <img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="/images/blog/ds01-in.jpg" alt="The letters 'in' in italic and in regular">
</figure>

**One word, two styles.** On every line, the "in" is set in italic and the rest of the word in regular. You may not consciously notice it, but it's exactly this <mark>subtle variation</mark> that makes the cover feel <mark>refined</mark>.

## Structure

**Logic.** The cover uses a single sentence pattern, "intime + section name", over and over. The left side stays fixed and carries <mark>brand recognition</mark>; the right side changes on every line and <mark>carries the content</mark>. One structure with several kinds of variation (word, typeface, case) creates a <mark>rhythm</mark>, much like music.

**Visual weight.** The longest, heaviest words sit near the central axis. The repeated "intime" on the left forms a vertical line of rhythm, and the vertical logo on the right breaks the symmetry.

**Spacing.** The leading between the big lines is very tight. It serves rhythm, not readability. The tiny lines of text threaded between them become the rests in the score.

## Logos and balance

<figure class="ds-fig ds-narrow">
  <img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="/images/blog/ds01-logos.jpg" alt="The cover with the headline dimmed, leaving the two logos and the small text visible">
</figure>

**Magazine logo, right.** A white circle with "intime" set vertically, near the horizontal midline. The circle breaks the straight lines of the page, and the reading direction turns from horizontal to vertical: a <mark>visual surprise</mark> inside a regular rhythm.

**Airport group logo, bottom left.** Small, low and quiet in colour, but it forms a <mark>diagonal counterweight</mark> to the logo on the right, pulling the visual weight back so the cover doesn't tip over.

**Overall balance.** The type is packed to the left, with a round logo on the right and a square one at bottom left: one high, one low, one round, one square. Together they form the cover's <mark>structural skeleton</mark>.
