Maison Martin Margiela's show invitations rarely take the form of a letter. Sometimes the invitation is a plate, sometimes a Rubik's cube, sometimes a raincoat…

<figure class="ds-fig">
  <img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="/images/blog/ds02-objects.jpg" alt="Three Margiela invitations: a printed plate, a Rubik's cube and a plastic raincoat">
</figure>

When we talk about designing an invitation, we shouldn't limit ourselves to designing a piece of mail. Anything can be an invitation. **What is really being designed is the interaction between the guest and the "invitation".**

So go back to the very first show, Spring/Summer 1989. How would you design the brand's first invitation?

## The brief

Imagine you've been reborn, and in this life your job is to design the invitation to this legendary brand's debut show. Here is what you have to work with: the design context.

### ① It's the brand's first show, so the invitation must set the tone: "who I am"

This is Maison Martin Margiela's first public appearance anywhere. The invitation is the first object from the brand that the outside world will touch, before any garment. Before the guests even walk into the venue, it has to establish the brand's whole attitude: **rebellious, deconstructed, anonymous, anti-luxury.**

<p class="ds-turn">But it can't read like a manifesto. It can't, say, print "I am rebelling" in a carefully chosen typeface, because choosing a typeface for an invitation is itself not rebellious.</p>

<p class="ds-need"><span>So the invitation needs to</span> <mark>declare "I am rebelling" without ever writing it.</mark></p>

### ② It has to actually get people to come: "you should be there"

Functionally, a show invitation has a few jobs: give the time, the place and the brand name, and make it clear that this is a serious professional invitation, not a joke or a piece of performance art. The recipients are all fashion editors, buyers and press. They receive dozens of invitations every season, and they are very good at "reading" the paper, type and craft to judge whether an event is worth their time.

<p class="ds-turn">There are two traps. Go "refined", with carefully chosen paper and type, and you are still speaking the language of polish: the recipient thinks "another refined label" and can't read anything distinctive about the brand. Go "wild", printing the show details on crumpled paper, and the recipient thinks "this doesn't respect me" and drops it in the bin.</p>

<p class="ds-need"><span>So the invitation needs to</span> <mark>make the recipient take it seriously, and feel the brand's rebellious spirit directly.</mark></p>

### ③ The brand has little money, but can't show it: "I chose this"

Margiela and his business partner Jenny Meirens spent nearly a year preparing the debut with no outside investment. The budget was extremely tight.

<p class="ds-turn">But if the invitation looks cheap, every stance above collapses. People will think you didn't "choose simplicity"; you "ran out of money and had to keep it simple".</p>

<p class="ds-need"><span>So the invitation needs to</span> <mark>cost very little, yet make the cheapness feel like a deliberate choice.</mark></p>

## The answer: a telegram

<figure class="ds-fig">
  <img loading="lazy" decoding="async" src="/images/blog/ds02-telegram.jpg" alt="The telegram invitation to Maison Martin Margiela's first show, with its blue envelope">
</figure>

Maison Martin Margiela's debut invitation went out to the fashion world <mark>as a telegram</mark>.

Unlike the refined, elaborate invitations other houses sent at the time, a telegram is an existing, standardised format of communication. Margiela didn't "design" an invitation; he "chose" a medium. The distinction matters: designing is expression, choosing is a stance. The choice itself carries the brand's rebellion.

At the same time, people's instinctive attitude towards a telegram is "this is a serious, official document that needs careful reading". That guaranteed the recipients would take the invitation seriously.

Finally, a conventional invitation spends its money on what is seen: layout, printing, paper. A telegram's cost goes only into transmission. You pay for the content, not the form. The brand spent the least money to send the strongest message.

<div class="ds-closing">
  <p>Margiela didn't design an invitation. He designed the confusion, the curiosity and the seriousness of the moment someone received it.</p>
  <p>In the end, design isn't about making an object. Posters, products and interfaces are only the medium.</p>
  <p>What design really does is talk to people, through objects, across time and space.</p>
