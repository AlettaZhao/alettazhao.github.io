<figure class="ds-fig">
  <img src="/images/blog/ds03-tickets.jpg" alt="Left: a Milan ATM paper metro ticket. Right: the reloadable 'ricaricami' card with Milano Cortina 2026 logos">
  <figcaption><strong>Left:</strong> a standard Milan metro paper ticket. <strong>Right:</strong> the reloadable card issued for the Milano Cortina 2026 Winter Olympics.</figcaption>
</figure>

Both do exactly the same thing: they get you on the metro. Yet they look nothing alike.

The visual gap is obvious, but the interesting part isn't how they look. It's how you treat them. The paper ticket goes in the bin once it's used; the card goes into your wallet and comes home with you. Both are tickets, yet one is treated as a consumable and the other as something worth keeping.

That difference is made by design. I call whatever creates it an object's "dignity".

By dignity I mean a relationship, built through design, between an object and the person using it: a sense that <mark>this is worth taking seriously</mark>.

Below, I break down <mark>four dimensions</mark> of <mark>how designers build dignity into an object</mark>.

## ① Being allowed something extra

<p class="ds-principle"><span>Principle</span> An object begins to have dignity when it is allowed to have something extra.</p>

<figure class="ds-fig">
  <img src="/images/blog/ds03-anatomy.jpg" alt="Annotated tickets: every element on the paper ticket is functional; the card adds a name, logos and a green band">
</figure>

On the paper ticket, not a single element is extra. Every element is a legal or functional requirement: ticket type, fare, where it's valid, restrictions. Its design freedom is zero. That's not to say nobody paid for the design; it means the designer had no room to decide anything. Everything on it is something it *must* have; nothing is something it *may* have.

The Olympic logos on the card, the name "ricaricami", the green band: all of these are functionally "unnecessary". And it is precisely these unnecessary things that give it dignity.

That "extra" can be a name, a symbol, a detail that serves no function at all. The message it sends is: someone cared that this thing is not just usable, but worth existing.

Conversely, when design is squeezed down to nothing but function, the object is stripped of its dignity.

Usually that isn't the designer's failure but a decision made by the system. Who decided this object doesn't "deserve" anything extra?

## ② Being rethought

<p class="ds-principle"><span>Principle</span> For an object to have dignity, someone must have seriously rethought it at least once.</p>

<figure class="ds-fig">
  <img src="/images/blog/ds03-ordinario.jpg" alt="Front and back of an older ATM paper ticket, almost identical in design">
</figure>

The paper ticket's material, printing and dimensions have barely changed in nearly twenty years (2006 to 2025). Its bare-bones look is less a design decision than inertia: nobody thought it worth redesigning.

That, too, is a loss of dignity. Not being designed badly on purpose, but being forgotten, left outside design's field of view.

Many objects lose their dignity not because they were badly designed, but because at some point nobody looked again at how they exist. Hospital wristbands, supermarket receipts, administrative forms: their design may be frozen in some version from fifteen years ago, and since then nobody has thought them "worth" redesigning.

## ③ How the object speaks

<p class="ds-principle"><span>Principle</span> Through the tone of its text, an object defines the power relationship with the person using it.</p>

<figure class="ds-fig">
  <img src="/images/blog/ds03-voice.jpg" alt="Text on the tickets: 'NON VALIDO per RHO FIERA', 'NON PIEGARE IL BIGLIETTO E NON AVVICINARLO A FONTI MAGNETICHE', and 'ricaricami'">
</figure>

This is a design dimension that rarely gets discussed.

The paper ticket speaks in commands and negatives: "NON PIEGARE" (do not fold), "NON AVVICINARLO A FONTI MAGNETICHE" (keep away from magnetic sources), "NON VALIDO per RHO FIERA" (not valid to Rho Fiera). It keeps telling you: don't do this, don't do that.

The card says "ricaricami": recharge me. That's an invitation, even a request. It sets up an equal relationship: you need me to ride, I need you to top me up. We're partners.

When an object is covered in prohibitions and negatives, what it's really saying is "I'm fragile, you might break me, and that would be your fault." That isn't dignity. It's anxiety.

## ④ A recognisable identity

<p class="ds-principle"><span>Principle</span> Dignity needs recognisability, but recognisability is not decoration.</p>

<figure class="ds-fig ds-narrow">
  <img src="/images/blog/ds03-five.jpg" alt="Five old ATM paper tickets laid out together, nearly indistinguishable">
</figure>

Put five old tickets side by side and you have to read the text carefully to tell what each one is for. Visually they are almost the same object; the differences live entirely in the text. These tickets have no "face". They are anonymous, indistinguishable from one another.

It resembles the logic of a uniform, which strips away individual identity to strengthen a sense of belonging to a system. But the old tickets don't even manage that sense of belonging. The ATM logo is merely repeated, mechanically, with no supporting colour, layout or hierarchy to turn it into a complete visual language. There is no real visual system. An object that disappears completely among its own kind does not, in a sense, "exist".

The card, by contrast, has a clear visual identity: a green palette, icons for every mode of transport, the Olympic co-branding. You recognise it at a glance.

Recognisability can come from a colour system, from proportions, from the choice of material: anything that makes the object "rise out of the background".

<div class="ds-summary">
  <div class="ds-summary-label">In sum</div>
  <p>All four principles point to the same thing: dignity is not a style. It is the material expression of a designer's belief that this object deserves to be taken seriously.</p>
  <p>Behind every principle lies a prior judgement: someone decided this object was worth the extra thought. Dignity doesn't appear on an object out of nowhere. It is a choice, made by the designer and by the decision-makers behind them.</p>
</div>

## A last thought: does dignity have an ethical limit?

The card's Olympic co-branding gives it the quality of a souvenir: you went to the Milan Olympics, and you brought this card home. Which means part of its dignity comes from the brand value of a commercial event.

That raises a question. Without the Olympic logos, how much design "decency" would the card have left? If not much, is its dignity its own, or borrowed?

For designers this is a very practical question. Co-branding, IP collaborations, limited editions: are these strategies giving objects dignity, or exposing how little dignity the objects have on their own?
